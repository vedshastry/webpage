About

I am a Ph.D. student in Economics at the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC), with research interests in environmental, development and applied microeconomics.
As an economist, my goal is to enhance our understanding of how long-term climate and technological change impact economic activity in the developing world.

In this pursuit, I particularly focus on agricultural markets, land use, and structural transformation. My current research shows the long-run impacts of diurnal warming (asymmetric changes in nighttime and daytime temperatures) on agricultural labour in India. I am also interested in how the growing demand for digital infrastructure interacts with natural resources and food–water–energy systems in developing economies.

Prior to (and during) grad school, I have provided research consulting/assistance on several projects in economics and public policy, spanning data centers in the U.S. (with Amazon), home-grown school meals in Burundi (with the World Bank), and irrigation, rural roads & agricultural markets in India (with the Indian School of Business).

Working Papers

  • Night and Day: Diurnal Warming and Long-run Structural Transformation in India

    [arXiv Paper] [Supplemental/Online Appendix]
    This paper shows how the composition of local economic activity shifts decadally under diurnal warming—higher nighttime and daytime temperatures— within Indian districts (1981–2011). Warmer nights raise, warmer days lower agricultural wage-labour shares. Though warmer days and nights both contract grain production and area under cultivation, only warmer days raise local harvest prices. Long differences show the labour divergence is within the rural population. In towns, both margins depress non-agricultural worker shares. These results are consistent with a model where warmer days shock land and labour productivity, but warmer nights are land-biased shocks in agriculture.

The (in)famous quip goes "those who can, do; those who can't, teach".
I beg to differ: one cannot "teach" something without knowing how to "do" it.

I have been fortunate to have found a kind and inspirational teacher at all stages in my life, and try my best to pass it on. It is my privilege to have taught (and continue teaching) mathematics and economics from grade school through the undergraduate, MBA and Ph.D. levels.

My primary goal as a teacher is to raise the floor of understanding in the classroom. I believe this aspect can often be overlooked in the quantitative world, which tends to prioritize the ceiling.
